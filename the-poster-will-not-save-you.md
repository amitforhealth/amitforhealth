Walk into almost any government medical college in India and you will find it bolted near the casualty entrance: a laminated board listing the sections of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and the punishments for assaulting a doctor. Three months here, two years there, a fine of five or ten thousand rupees. It hangs with the quiet confidence of a job done. To the second-year resident walking past it at 3am, toward a corridor already filling with a dead man’s relatives, it is the most honest document in the building, because it advertises a protection that does not exist.

[“The Wages of Fear”](the-wages-of-fear) argued that violence against doctors is, in the end, violence against patients: every beaten doctor teaches a hundred others to flinch, and the flinch is paid for by the sickest Indian with nowhere else to go. That was the diagnosis. This is the prescription. And it begins by admitting that the poster is a lie.

## The arithmetic the mob has already done

More than nineteen states have passed laws specifically protecting healthcare workers. The number of convictions those laws have produced, across years and thousands of incidents, is indistinguishable from zero. In Maharashtra in 2019, of 157 recorded attacks on medical staff, 77 were reported, 71 were charge-sheeted, two reached a verdict, and not one ended in conviction. Hundreds of cases registered nationwide in recent years have yielded no conviction worth the name. The mob has run the calculation the courts will not: breaking a doctor in India costs, in practice, nothing.

Now contrast the speed. When the state decides someone is a criminal, it can move with astonishing velocity. We have watched bulldozers reach an accused man’s home before the chargesheet does. We have seen swift, visible, theatrical enforcement deployed for rioters and land-grabbers. The machinery of deterrence exists; it is simply never pointed at the man who fractures a resident’s jaw. Whatever one thinks of bulldozer justice, its message is unmistakable, and the doctor in the casualty ward has noticed that the message is never sent on her behalf.

## Ask the uncomfortable question

Are doctors not a vote bank? Is their association too polite, too fractured, too apologetic to frighten anyone? A press reporter is given police protection. A small-town politician travels with gunmen. The wealthy buy private security details and air-conditioned waiting rooms. But the woman holding a ventilator together in a district hospital, treating the poorest Indians under the worst conditions, is told to take comfort from a sign on the wall. By any honest measure she is a national asset. She is guarded like an afterthought.

## What supporting doctors actually means

It means the same thing as supporting patients, and it comes down to three demands. 

**First, punishment that is real, not printed.** An assault on on-duty medical staff must trigger an FIR within the hour, a non-bailable charge, a fast-tracked trial, and — the part that actually matters — a visible conviction. Not two dozen. A handful, publicised hard, will empty a casualty corridor of fists faster than any statute gathering dust. Deterrence is not the severity printed on the wall; it is the certainty understood in the street.

**Second, protection that is physical, not laminated.** If the state can post armed police outside the bungalows of the comfortable, it can post four or five at the emergency and intensive-care wards of every government hospital and medical college — the precise places where violence concentrates after dark. A camera records an assault. A constable prevents it. We have spent on the first and starved the second.

**Third, treat the government doctor as the asset she is.** The resident who chooses the casualty ward over a safe private clinic is doing the republic a favour it has not earned. Pay her, staff her shift, give her a trained officer to face the families, and protect her body as seriously as we protect a legislator’s. Do this and fewer of her batchmates will price assault into their choice of specialty; do this and fewer will board the flight to a country that does not beat its healers.

## The closing argument

None of this asks for sympathy. The doctors of India are not asking to be loved. They are asking for the bare minimum a state owes anyone it sends, unarmed, into the most dangerous room in the building: that when they are attacked, something happens to the attacker. A right that is never enforced is not a right. It is a press release in a frame.

So take the poster down. Or better — leave it up, and finally make it true. Because the doctor that poster fails to protect is the same one who will be on duty when the patient carried into that corridor next is *yours*.
