When doctors are attacked

The man who beat his own hospital

In Dombivli, an elected corporator assaulted doctors for not having a bed his own corporation never provided. The CCTV also caught the reason nothing will change.

July 2026 · 6 min read

On the evening of July 6th, in the Kalyan-Dombivli Municipal Corporation’s Shastri Nagar Hospital, a newborn needed intensive care and the neonatal ICU had no vacant bed. The doctors on duty did what medicine and honesty required: they told the family the truth, and advised that the infant be moved at once to a hospital that could keep it alive. The family did not call an ambulance first. It called a politician. Ramesh Mhatre, a Shiv Sena corporator—an elected member of the very civic body that runs the hospital—arrived with supporters, and the CCTV cameras recorded what followed: the Resident Medical Officer, Dr Vaibhav Salunkhe, assaulted; a woman doctor and nursing staff manhandled; the casualty area of a public hospital converted, once again, into a stage for the oldest performance in Indian healthcare.

Consider, precisely, what was being punished. Not negligence: the doctors had examined the child, recognised the limits of their unit and recommended the one intervention actually available—transfer. Not indifference: the advice was itself the care. What was punished was arithmetic. The NICU had no bed. And a hospital’s bed count is not set by its residents at 3am; it is set by budgets, sanctioned posts and civic priorities—by, among others, the corporators of the Kalyan-Dombivli Municipal Corporation. Mr Mhatre did not storm past that CCTV camera to confront the shortage he and his colleagues preside over. He arrived to transfer it—onto the face of the nearest man in a white coat. It would be hard to design a cleaner demonstration: the state fails to fund a bed, and then the state’s own representative punishes a doctor for saying so out loud.

Readers of the two previous essays in this series will find the scene familiar. “The Wages of Fear” argued that the true victim of such violence is never chiefly the doctor who is struck but the patient not yet born into the system—because every beaten doctor teaches a hundred others to flinch, and the flinch, multiplied across an under-doctored country, is a body count. “The Poster Will Not Save You” argued that the remedy is not another statute but the enforcement of the ones that exist: a visible conviction, a guard at the door. Dombivli compresses both arguments into ninety seconds of video. The statistic that circulates with each fresh incident—that as many as 80% of Indian doctors face violence at work—no longer shocks anyone; it has become wallpaper. Which forces the question this essay is obliged to take up, because the incidents keep arriving on schedule while the essays pile up: why does nothing change?

The script everyone knows by heart

Watch what happened next, hour by hour, and notice that nobody needed a rehearsal. The clip went out on social media. The medical fraternity erupted. Resident doctors suspended routine services across Kalyan-Dombivli while keeping emergency care running—a courtesy the mob never extends to them. At Nair Hospital in Mumbai, doctors pinned black ribbons to their coats. MARD, the residents’ association, announced letters to the Public Health Department and the Ministry of Home Affairs. Under the pressure of the visuals, four men were arrested; Mr Mhatre himself surrendered on July 8th, two days after the assault—surrendered, note, at a moment of his choosing, in the manner of a man managing a schedule rather than fleeing a law. There will now follow assurances, perhaps a committee, possibly an announcement about security guards. Then bail. Then quiet. Then, in some other district, another corridor, the same play with a new cast.

It is tempting to call this a failure of the system. It is more accurate to call it the system. The cycle persists because it pays every participant except one. The family receives catharsis and the sense that someone was made to answer. The politician receives the most valuable currency in municipal politics: a video of himself “taking action” on behalf of a voter. The association receives a day of visible anger that costs the government nothing. The administration receives credit for swift arrests that will curdle into acquittals long after the cameras leave. The press receives a clip. Outrage is thus accepted, by all sides, as payment in lieu of reform—a currency infinitely easier to print than a NICU bed. The only party who pays true value is off-screen: the next newborn, the next 3am resident, the next family whose relative dies in transit because a doctor somewhere learned the price of an honest referral.

The record supports the cynicism, unfortunately, in full. After the rape and murder of a trainee doctor at Kolkata’s R.G. Kar hospital in August 2024—two years ago next month—the country witnessed the largest doctors’ agitation in its history, a suo-motu intervention by the Supreme Court and a National Task Force on the safety of medical professionals. The task force concluded that no separate central law was needed. In March 2025 the health ministry confirmed to Parliament that no central protection law was in the pipeline; the states, it noted, have their own. Maharashtra does indeed have its own—the Medicare Act, in force since 2010. Its yield was examined in the previous essay and bears repeating like a tolling bell: of 157 recorded attacks on medical staff in the state in 2019, not one produced a conviction. Every actor in the Dombivli drama knew this arithmetic. That is why the play proceeds so smoothly. Impunity is not a loophole in the script. It is the script.

What memory would do

The previous essay asked for enforcement, and the demand stands. But Dombivli suggests the missing ingredient is something logically prior to enforcement: memory. The mob’s calculation rests on a single assumption—that everyone will forget. The clip fades in a week; the black ribbons come off; the association’s letter joins its predecessors in a file; the case dissolves into adjournments no reporter attends. Deterrence does not die in the courtroom. It dies in the amnesia between hearings.

So the discipline worth building is the unglamorous one. Let the medical associations publish and maintain a public ledger—every assault on a healthcare worker in the state: FIR number, sections applied, charge-sheet date, next hearing, current status—and let the ledger, not the ribbon, be the protest. Let MARD’s letters to the ministries name not just the demand but the date on which the association will publicly report the answer it received. Let journalists mark the anniversary, not merely the atrocity: the story headlined “Dombivli, six months on” is worth more to the next resident than a thousand sympathetic editorials this week. And let this case, precisely because the accused is an elected official caught on camera, be pushed the whole distance. One conviction of one corporator would do more to empty the casualty corridors of fists than every laminated poster in Maharashtra. The state has been asked for many things by its doctors. This asks something of the rest of us: the follow-up question, asked out loud, after the outrage has become unfashionable.

And the newborn—the child in whose name the fists were thrown? The transfer the doctors advised was the only medicine on offer in that hospital that evening. The assault added nothing to that child’s chances; it merely subtracted from every future child’s. Somewhere in Maharashtra a young resident watched that CCTV clip this week and quietly revised the price of honesty—recalculated what it costs, at 3am, to tell a desperate family the truth about beds. That revision, not anything a magistrate eventually rules, is the real verdict in the Dombivli case, and it was delivered within hours. The last essay ended by asking that the poster on the casualty wall be made true. This one ends with something smaller and harder. Remember the date: July 6th. Ask, in six months, what became of the case. If the answer is “nothing”—as it was for all 157 attacks Maharashtra recorded in a single year—then at least the forgetting, this time, will have been a choice.
